\section*{Proposal 1: Through-Wall Motion Radar}

\subsection*{High-Level Description}

A portable radar that shows you people moving on the other side of a wall. The finished device is a box you hold in one hand (may be unrealistic, backup is two hands). Ideally we'd get the box to be around the size of a thick phone, but that may be unrealistic based on the antenna arrangement we will need to adopt. A patch antenna array points to the "top" of the box, a screen on the front showing a forward sector view. You point it at a wall, press a button, and moving people behind that wall appear as blips that track as they walk around. 

Commercial through-wall radars exist. The Range-R unit used by US police and the Xaver line are two examples, but they cost between six and fifty thousand dollars (The Range-R website asks for a quote, that's how you know its expensive). The pitch is that a student team can build a functional one for a few hundred dollars in parts, that's excluding our designs and RnD cost. The users who care are first responders searching the ruins of a collapsed building or tactical teams clearing a structure, both teams would want to know whether a room is occupied before they walk into it. 

The device works by sending out a radio signal and listening for the echo, the same idea as a bat using echolocation. The delay before the echo comes back tells us how far away something is, and comparing the echo across a few antennas in some arrangement tells us roughly which direction it came from. The wall, the floor, and the furniture all bounce back the same echo every time, so we tune the device to ignore anything that isn't changing and only highlight signals that shift from one moment to the next, which is what a walking person looks like. Those changing echoes become the blips on the screen.

\subsection*{Implementation}

Some definitions for you the reader and also as notes for myself as I research:

\textbf{PLL}: phase-locked loop, the controller chip that commands and stabilizes the signal sweep.

\textbf{VCO}: voltage-controlled oscillator, the part that generates the RF signal, controlled by the PLL.

\textbf{Mixer}: combines the received echo with the outgoing chirp to produce a much lower frequency that encodes range.

\textbf{LNA}: low-noise amplifier, boosts the faint echo signal

\subsubsection*{Implementation Issues}

For the processor board we're leaning toward a SBC like a Raspberry Pi 5, with a cheap Jetson as a stretch, rather than an FPGA. The biggest computational load we have to worry about is whether the processer is fast enough to run several FFTs in parallel. If we end up scaling to more RX channels and need more throughput, a Jetson Orin Nano is the likely the next choice. However, if someone on the team happens to want to do FPGA work, that could be a stretch goal too. 

Since RF design is unforgiving, and I suspect no one on the team that will work on this project has any RF expereince, the mechanical design of the arrange of transmitters and receivers as well as the algorithms used to parse it into something readable will be by far the most difficult part of the implementation. A lot of time and effort, and possibly money, will have to go into making this work. The team should also work as quick as possible to have a proof of concept working. If the team is unable to do so, they will need to consider pivoting to an alternate project.

FMCW radar in this band is otherwise well-trodden ground. MIT's 6.S062 ``coffee-can radar'' and Henrik Forsten's open 6 GHz FMCW radar project are both public end-to-end designs, and parts like the ADF4159 PLL paired with the HMC series are available at reasonable cost. MIT's WiTrack project already pulled off through-wall localization of a single moving person in roughly this band with about 1.8 GHz of bandwidth\footnote{\url{https://witrack.csail.mit.edu/}}\footnote{\url{https://witrack.csail.mit.edu/witrack-paper.pdf}}, so we're re-implementing a known result on a budget, not inventing a new one. Their follow-up, WiTrack2.0, has useful collected data and antenna array layouts to reference as we design our own\footnote{\url{https://witrack.csail.mit.edu/witrack2-paper.pdf}}. The antenna array, the moving-target indication through a real wall, and getting the pipeline to run in real time are the hard parts for us, but none of them is an open research question.

The device only sees moving people, since the static subtraction erases anyone holding perfectly still. A breathing-detection mode is a stretch goal that would recover some of them: a still person's chest still moves as they breathe, and MIT's Vital-Radio project has shown breathing and heart rate can be picked out through a wall from radio reflections alone\footnote{\url{https://www.mit.edu/~fadel/papers/vitalradio-paper.pdf}}, so it is a demonstrated result rather than something we would be inventing.

Wall material changes how fast the signal travels through it, which means different types of walls are going to yield different distances. For the thin interior walls we're initially targeting (drywall, plywood), that bias is on the order of a couple of centimeters, which is to say fine and negligible. Thicker solid walls like brick add tens of centimeters, which is part of why they're not explicitly in scope. For a bonus demo, we could demo the device through like the outer wall of EECS, but we're likely just going to run with the decreased accuracy. 

Full imaging, AKA getting an actual silhouette or skeleton, is not achievable for this project. MIT's RF-Pose needed a trained neural network and camera-synchronized source to get there\footnote{\url{https://rfpose.csail.mit.edu/}}, and that's years of work. 

\subsection*{Evaluation and Testing}

Our minimum bar for success is to detect a single person walking behind one interior drywall, range error under about 50 cm, and tracks the person's motion somewhat accurately. AKA, the blip on the screen should track the person as they walk around in real time.

For more rigorous testing, we can have a person walks a marked path in the far room while a camera in that room records. We compare the estimated track against the actual one and extract detection range, range accuracy, angular accuracy, refresh rate, and how many people we can resolve at once.

\section*{Proposal 2 (Unrealistic): Head-Mounted Aurora Viewer}

\subsection*{High-Level Description}

A visor that lets you see auroae the naked eye cannot. The human eye integrates light over roughly a tenth of a second, so a faint aurora appears as a dim grey smudge with no color. A camera can integrate for five seconds and pull out bright, structured green and purple curtains. This device performs that integration live and puts the result on a display in front of your eyes.

This idea originated when I first went aurora viewing here in Michigan, and was confused when everyone else began pulling out thier phones and cameras to take pictures while I could not see anything in the sky. I was able to capture the faint aurora with a long exposure shot on my phone, but I thought being able to view the faint and otherwise invisible aurora in real time would be very cool. 

The finished device is a visor or a pair of glasses. A low-light camera like the type used in dash cams (some fancy Sony sensor that works well with motion and low light) at the top, two micro-OLED displays, an IMU on the frame, and a compute pack on the headband or belt worn around the waist. It captures frames continuously, uses the IMU together with the camera stream to register each new frame against the running stack so that head motion cancels out, and integrates about five seconds of exposure. The stacked, denoised, color-boosted image is sent to the displays at a the highest framerate we can get, which will probably be in the 1 to low 2 digits. The compute pack (probably something along the lines of a Jetson Orin) may be quite heavy, hence why we suggest it could be waist mounted.

\subsubsection*{Implementation Issues}

The compute module runs hot and draws 2 digit wattage constantly, so we will need a big battery and some active cooling. 

Optics and camera selection is very finicky and iterative. There will be an extreme amount of sensor tuning and experimentation with filters to get an actually usable output. There's also the question of using the camera stream to do stabilization, which means some sort of sensor fusion algorithm, which is difficult and finicky to implement too. 

CV will be a very significant part of this project, so much so that you could argue that it becomes a ML project, not an embedded project.

\subsection*{Evaluation and Testing}

We would compare the stacked output's signal-to-noise ratio against a single frame, and against a tripod-mounted long-exposure camera shooting the same patch of sky. 

Success is pulling a Kp 3 aurora into clear, colored visibility when the naked eye can barely see anything. As a convenient indoor test, we can project a dim, slowly varying synthetic aurora onto a screen in a dark room and confirm the device recovers it while a bare camera frame does not. 

Eventually, real world testing should ideally be done. I captured my aurora at Pinckney Recreation Area about 30 minutes North by car of Ann Arbor. Go when there are clear skies and a high enough Kp value reported by NOAA.
