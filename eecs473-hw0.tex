\section*{Proposal 1: Through-Wall Motion Radar}

\subsection*{high-level description}

a portable radar that shows you people moving on the other side of a wall. the
finished device is a box you hold or set on a tripod: a patch antenna array on
the front face, a screen on the back showing a top-down view, a battery, and a
handle. point it at a wall and moving people behind that wall show up as blips
that track as they walk around.

commercial through-wall radars exist (the Range-R that US police use, the Xaver
line) but they run six to fifty thousand dollars. the pitch is that a student
team can build a functional one for a few hundred dollars in parts. the users
who care are first responders doing search and rescue in a collapsed or smoke
filled building, and tactical teams clearing a structure, both of whom want to
know if a room is occupied before they walk into it. past that it is a strong
demo of what RF sensing can actually do, which is why we think it draws a crowd
at the expo.

how it works: we build our own FMCW (frequency modulated continuous wave) radar
front end operating somewhere in the 3 to 7 GHz range. the transmitter sends a
chirp, the receiver mixes the return against the outgoing signal, and the beat
frequency of that mix is proportional to target range. multiple receive
antennas give us angle of arrival by comparing phase across the array. the walls
and furniture are static, so we subtract a running background estimate (moving
target indication) and what is left is the moving people. we render those as
blips on the top-down display.

we picked this band deliberately. 60 GHz mmWave radar modules are cheap and off
the shelf but 60 GHz does not go through walls, the attenuation is enormous.
lower frequencies penetrate drywall, plywood and brick with a manageable loss,
and 3 to 7 GHz is the range where you still get enough bandwidth for useful
range resolution. MIT's WiTrack demonstrated through-wall localization of a
single moving person in roughly this band with about 1.8 GHz of bandwidth, so
the physics is already proven. we are re-implementing a known result on a
budget, not inventing a new one.

\subsection*{implementation issues}

\textit{rough plan for the design expo.} figuring there is about a ten day lag
between ordering a PCB and getting it, and that RF boards usually take more than
one respin, the schedule is roughly:

\begin{enumerate}
\item planning and part selection (weeks 1 to 3). pick the PLL/VCO, mixer, LNA,
ADC and processor. simulate the antenna array. settle on chirp bandwidth and
rate.
\item breadboard the RF chain on eval modules (weeks 3 to 6). one transmit, one
receive. get a clean range measurement in free space and stream raw IQ samples
to a laptop.
\item PCB design and first order (weeks 5 to 8). RF front end plus processor
plus power on a controlled-impedance board, with footprints for the full
receive array. order by roughly 10/26.
\item DSP pipeline on the laptop (weeks 6 to 10). FMCW range processing, moving
target indication, angle of arrival, top-down visualization. validate it
through a real interior wall.
\item board bring-up and integration (weeks 10 to 12). move the DSP onto the
processor (or stream to a companion module), integrate the screen and
enclosure, second board respin if needed.
\item final testing, calibration, poster, expo (weeks 12 to 14). characterize
range and accuracy, add stretch goals if time allows.
\end{enumerate}

\textit{rough budget.} across a team of five the ceiling is \$1250.

\begin{itemize}
\item RF parts (PLL/VCO, mixers, LNAs, ADC): \textasciitilde\$150
\item PCB manufacture, controlled impedance and likely a Rogers laminate for the
RF section, two or three respins: \textasciitilde\$350
\item antennas, connectors, semi-rigid cable: \textasciitilde\$100
\item processor / FPGA dev board and screen: \textasciitilde\$100
\item enclosure, battery, misc: \textasciitilde\$100
\end{itemize}

that is around \$800, comfortably under budget. the RF-grade PCB is the cost
driver and the item most likely to blow up, since every respin is another board
order and another ten day wait.

\textit{other implementation issues.} RF is unforgiving and the antenna plus
front end is the long pole of the whole project, we are budgeting for at least
two board spins because of it. we need bench access to a VNA and a spectrum
analyzer for bring-up, which the department lab has. on the regulatory side, we
stay inside the FCC Part 15 UWB emission mask: transmit power stays very low, no
license required. building a high power radar in a licensed band is not on the
table. there are no moving parts and no mechanical hazards. the energy budget is
fine, the transmit power is tiny and the display and processor are the real
draw.

\textit{why we think it is doable.} FMCW radar in this frequency range is a well
documented space. MIT's 6.S062 "coffee can radar" and Henrik Forsten's open 6
GHz FMCW radar project are both public end-to-end designs, and integrated
PLL/VCO parts like the ADF4159 paired with the HMC series exist at reasonable
cost. the hard, novel work for us is the antenna array, the moving target
indication through a real wall, and getting the pipeline to run in real time.
none of those is an open research question, they are just work, and this team
can do a lot of work.

\subsection*{evaluation and testing}

the MVP measure of success: detect and range a single person walking behind one
interior drywall wall, with range error under about 50 cm. the v1 target: add
angle of arrival, so the device tracks that person's 2D position and the blip
moves on the screen in real time as they walk.

test method: a person walks a marked path in the far room while a camera in that
room, timestamp-synced to the radar, records ground truth. we compare the
estimated track against the actual track and pull out detection range, range
accuracy, angular accuracy, update rate, and how many people we can separate at
once.

real-world caveats we will be honest about in the report. the device only sees
moving people, because the static subtraction erases anyone holding perfectly
still (a breathing-detection mode is a stretch goal that gets some of them
back). wall material changes the propagation speed, so range needs a per-wall
calibration knob, we are not going to pretend it is plug and play. multiple
walls and exterior brick are out of scope. and full imaging, an actual
silhouette or skeleton, is explicitly not what this is, that is years of work
and a trained neural net, we track blips.

\section*{Proposal 2 (wacky): Head-Mounted Aurora Amplifier}

\subsection*{high-level description}

a visor that lets you see auroae the naked eye can't. the human eye integrates
light over roughly a tenth of a second, so a faint aurora reads as a dim grey
smudge with no color. a camera can integrate for five seconds and pull out
bright structured green and purple curtains. this device does that integration
live and puts the result on a display in front of your eyes.

the finished thing is a visor: a low-light camera up top, two micro-OLED
displays, an IMU on the frame, and a compute pack on the headband or belt. it
grabs frames continuously, uses the IMU plus optical flow to register each new
frame against the running stack so your head motion cancels out, and integrates
about five seconds of exposure. the stacked, denoised, color-boosted image goes
to the displays at a few frames per second. users are aurora chasers and
astro-tourists who drove somewhere cold and want to actually see the show, plus
photographers who want a live preview instead of chimping the back of a DSLR.

\subsection*{why this one is unrealistic for us}

the hard part is real-time multi-frame registration and stacking on a wearable
power budget, and that is a computer vision and ML problem, not an embedded
systems one. doing it well needs a Jetson-class module, careful low-light
denoising, and solid motion estimation, and if we lean on a COTS compute module
for all of that then our PCB is basically a carrier board with an IMU and a
display driver, which undersells the "design a PCB around a processor" goal of
the course. the low-light camera and optics selection is also finicky, and
aurora only happens on cold clear nights with low light pollution and decent
geomagnetic activity, so the test loop is brutal, we would be driving north of
the city at 1am hoping for a KP 4.

\subsection*{if we were forced to scope it}

COTS low-light camera, Jetson Orin Nano, IMU. prototype the whole stacking
pipeline on a laptop against recorded night-sky and aurora footage first, then
port it to the Jetson. the PCB is a carrier: power regulation, IMU, display
drivers, camera interface.

rough budget: Jetson \textasciitilde\$250, low-light camera
\textasciitilde\$100, micro-OLED displays \textasciitilde\$150, IMU and passives
\textasciitilde\$50, optics and enclosure \textasciitilde\$100. around \$650,
dominated by the compute module.

evaluation: compare the stacked output's signal-to-noise against a single frame,
and against a tripod-mounted long-exposure DSLR shooting the same patch of sky.
success is pulling a KP 3 aurora into clear colored visibility when the naked
eye sees a faint grey smudge.
